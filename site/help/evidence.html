Evidence and references

Nothing on this site is a medical treatment, and no session is sold as one. This page is the other half of that promise: what is established about beats, resonances and paced breathing, what is contested, what is folklore, and what has been shown to be false. Every claim the studio’s own copy makes appears below with its status — and where a number could not be verified it is listed as unresolved rather than printed as fact.

The ledger holds 51 claims: 20 established · 8 mixed · 5 folk · 18 debunked. There are 40 references and 10 unresolved items.

How to read a status

  • established — measured, replicated or uncontroversial — safe to state plainly
  • mixed — real but contested, or true only in a narrow form — state the narrow form
  • folk — a tradition, a practice or a named set — say whose practice it is, never a fact about the body
  • debunked — the popular version is false — the studio must correct it or leave it out

Citations are written as author, title, outlet and year so they can be found by typing them into any search engine. This site carries no external links by design — nothing here phones out, and nothing here depends on a page elsewhere staying up.

What could not be verified

These came up in the research and could not be confirmed. Where a claim is unresolved the site omits the number or carries the uncertainty — it does not guess.

  1. The exact upper carrier limit for binaural beats (1000 Hz vs 1500 Hz). Oster's article is reported as saying binaural beats are not heard above a carrier of about 1000 Hz, while Hemi-Sync's own process paper cites Oster for a ~1500 Hz limit. Blocker: the article copy available was a re-typeset rendering (an OCR/transcribed PDF) whose upper-limit sentence is truncated mid-line. Safe wording: "binaural beats are heard best with low carriers and become indistinct in the kilohertz range."
  2. The ~3 dB beat depth figure. Quoted from a circulated summary of Oster rather than from the article's own tables. It is consistent with everything else known about binaural beat depth (monaural beats are much deeper), but it should be re-checked against the original before printing a number.
  3. Perceptual adaptation — "the beat fades after N minutes." The ASSR literature documents habituation, and beat research repeatedly notes lability, but no specific peer-reviewed study quantifying the fade-out of the binaural beat percept was located. Blocker: no dedicated search of the psychoacoustic adaptation literature was possible within this pass. Do not print a number of minutes.
  4. The origin of the 174, 285 and 963 Hz additions to the solfeggio list. They circulate universally and are attributed to "later authors", but no primary source was found. State them as later additions with unknown provenance.
  5. 528 Hz study provenance. The frequently cited "528 Hz DNA" experiment is not in the peer-reviewed record; the trail runs into 1990s self-published material. Do not name a study or an author on the site beyond "a 1990s alternative-medicine book".
  6. The exact Schumann amplitudes (picotesla / microvolts-per-metre). The order of magnitude is well established; the single illustrative figure should be re-checked against a specific measurement paper before printing.
  7. Monroe Institute EEG work by Campbell/McDonnell (1970s). Described in the Institute's own accounts as thousands of monitored sessions, but never published in peer-reviewed form; the descriptions come from secondary and promotional sources.
  8. Full-stereo/lossy-encoding degradation of binaural beats. Sound engineering reasoning, and repeated in hobbyist documentation, but no controlled study was located.
  9. Cognito Therapeutics phase 3 (HOPE) results. The trial began enrolling in December 2022 and enrolment completed; no peer-reviewed pivotal readout was located in this pass. Anything the site says about 40 Hz therapy must be dated and flagged as "as of 2026".
  10. The original USENET measurement's author, group and date. All copies found are republishings; the measurement lineage for Focus 22–27 therefore has no citable primary author, only the sbagen documentation that preserves it.

Safety, in one place

  • Volume. Conversation level is about 60 dB and is plenty. Long exposure above 80 dB is where hearing damage starts, and it is cumulative. The 60/60 rule for headphones: 60% volume, 60 minutes.
  • Seizure risk. Photosensitive epilepsy is real, and it applies to flicker — which this site does not use. If you have a seizure history, treat any rhythmic sensory stimulation with care and talk to your clinician first.
  • Tinnitus. Keep levels low. Loud tones can aggravate tinnitus rather than relieve it.
  • Driving, machinery, water. Do not use headphone sessions while doing any of these. A long session is easy to lose track of — the studio has its own timer for that.
  • Not treatment. Nothing here diagnoses, treats, cures or prevents anything. If you have a health concern, this is not a substitute for care.
